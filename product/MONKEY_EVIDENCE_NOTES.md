# Evidence notes for Mischief’s “Tiny science” lines

A086–A105 in `MONKEY_QUOTE_BANK.md` are **plain-language paraphrases**, not direct quotations from researchers. They are intentionally written conservatively so Mischief can be encouraging without pretending that a single study proves a universal rule.

## A086–A089 — implementation intentions

**Basis:** Peter M. Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran, “Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-analysis of Effects and Processes,” *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38 (2006), 69–119. DOI: `10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1`.

The review analyzed 94 independent tests and found that implementation intentions—specific plans linking a situation to an action, often described as “if situation Y occurs, then I will do X”—had a positive effect on goal attainment. These lines therefore talk about choosing the when/where/how and making the next cue/action clear. They do **not** claim that planning guarantees success.

## A090–A093 — habit formation and automaticity

**Basis:** Phillippa Lally, Cornelia H. M. van Jaarsveld, Henry W. W. Potts, and Jane Wardle, “How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world,” *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40 (2010), 998–1009. DOI: `10.1002/ejsp.674`.

Participants repeated a chosen behavior in a consistent context. Automaticity generally increased with repetition, but the time course varied widely between people. The study is also a useful antidote to the popular “21 days” myth. These lines therefore emphasize repetition and gradual automaticity without promising a fixed timeline.

## A094–A097 — self-efficacy and mastery experiences

**Basis:** Albert Bandura, Nancy E. Adams, and John Beyer, “Cognitive processes mediating behavioral change,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 35(3) (1977), 125–139. DOI: `10.1037/0022-3514.35.3.125`, together with Bandura’s later self-efficacy framework.

Self-efficacy theory treats mastery experiences—actually performing and succeeding at a task—as an important source of beliefs about one’s own capability. These lines turn that into the softer idea that confidence can sometimes follow experience rather than needing to arrive first. They do **not** claim that completing one task permanently rewires confidence.

## A098–A101 — small progress and motivation

**Basis:** Teresa M. Amabile and Steven J. Kramer’s research behind *The Progress Principle*, based on nearly 12,000 daily work diaries from knowledge workers. Their published summaries report that progress in meaningful work, even small progress, was strongly associated with positive inner work life and motivation.

These lines say that small progress can support motivation. They do **not** claim that every tiny action will automatically improve mood or performance.

## A102–A105 — attention residue and task switching

**Basis:** Sophie Leroy, “Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks,” *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 109(2) (2009), 168–181. DOI: `10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002`.

Across two experiments, switching away from a task could leave continuing cognitive activity related to the prior task, and performance on the next task could suffer. These lines therefore encourage staying with one task and capturing distracting thoughts rather than immediately switching. They do **not** claim multitasking is universally impossible or that every interruption has the same effect.

## Editorial rule

Do not add phrases such as “scientists proved,” “this rewires your brain,” “dopamine increases,” or numerical performance claims unless a source directly supports that exact statement. Prefer “research suggests,” “researchers found,” or “studies have found,” and keep the claim narrower than the underlying evidence rather than broader.
